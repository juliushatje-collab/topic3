01 · Theory

The theorem only holds if nobody else matters

Information is an option: if it does not help, you ignore it.

VOI=𝔼s[maxa𝔼[u(a,ω)∣s]]−maxa𝔼[u(a,ω)]≥0

You never pay for not getting information (Blackwell 1951).

The hidden assumption

Your payoff does not depend on what others know.
In a market it does: the price is made by everyone's information.

Hirshleifer (1971): in a game, public information can make every player worse off.

01 · Paper 1

The index investor never loses a lot

Schredelseker (2014), "Pascal's Wager and Information", Journal of Forecasting

market efficientmarket inefficient
I forecastwin nothingwin a lot
I don't forecastlose nothinglose a lot

The textbook logic

Forecasting weakly dominates, so it is rational whenever an inefficiency is possible.

The flaw

A non-forecaster (coin-flipper, index investor) never "loses a lot": he earns the market average in both columns.

The market is zero-sum against the average: not everyone who knows more than nothing can beat it.

03 · Herding

Rational herding: how a room loses its information

Information cascades: Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch (1992), Banerjee (1992)

P(A∣k)=2k2k+1

k = light minus dark balls you can infer. Two public "A", own ball dark: k = 2 − 1 = 1, so P(A) = 2/3. Say A.

In the lab

Anderson & Holt (1997) ran this exact game: rational cascades formed in most periods in which they could.

01 Rational

After two equal answers, the public history outweighs your own ball.

02 Uninformative

From then on, answers reveal nothing. Private information stops reaching the public.

03 Fragile

Built on two balls, the cascade can be wrong and can break on one new public signal.

Every player used more information than the one before, yet the room as a whole ended up knowing less. The coin market works the same way.

03 · Herding

Traders have good reasons to herd

A theory of why it pays, and the data that shows it

Froot, Scharfstein & Stein (1992): "Herd on the street"

A short-horizon speculator only profits if others trade on his information after him.

So it pays to study what everybody else studies, even information unrelated to fundamentals.

Information is valuable because others have it too.

03 · Public information

Can central bank transparency make markets worse?

Morris & Shin (2002): you want to be right, and close to what the others do (Keynes' beauty contest)

ai=(1−r)𝔼i[θ]+r𝔼i[a‾] weight on public signal=αα+β(1−r)>αα+β

α, β: precision of the public and the private signal. r: how much you care about the others.

Welfare falls with more precise public information only if β/α > 1 / ((1 − r)(2r − 1)), i.e. at least 8. Svensson (2006): so in practice, mostly pro transparency.

03 · Psychology

Active traders pay for their confidence

First: who here drives better than the average person in this room?

Confidence rises, accuracy does not

Oskamp (1965): psychologists and students read a case in four parts. After part 1: 26 % correct, 33 % confident. Confidence rose with every part, accuracy did not. At the end, over 90 % were overconfident.

Tsai, Klayman & Hastie (2008): same pattern with more cues; confidence grows faster than accuracy.

In markets

Odean (1998): overconfident traders trade too much and lower their own expected utility.

Barber & Odean (2000): 66,465 US households, 1991–96, net annual return

T2 in real life: a little information, a lot of confidence.

04 · Value of information

Half an analyst is worse than none

Value of buying coins, starting from T0 who knows nothing (all 1,024 sequences, everyone else fundamental)

Best choice at this price

For every price: 0 or 9 coins, never in between. The value of information is not concave (Radner & Stiglitz 1984).

Tested in Innsbruck

Huber, Angerer & Kirchler (2011): traders could buy one of five information levels. The uninformed earned the highest net returns; over time, people bought full information or none.

Grossman & Stiglitz (1980): with costly information, markets cannot be fully efficient.

Information only pays in bulk: the market splits into index investors and insiders.
